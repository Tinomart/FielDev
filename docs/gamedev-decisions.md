# Game development in FielDev: what was decided, what was tried, what is open

Written overnight, while nobody could be asked. Every decision below was made by me, with the alternative that was not taken, so that it can be changed in one place. Nothing here has been seen in a real window or on Windows: the application ran in a virtual display on a Linux machine, with software rendering, and the pictures and logs of those runs are the only evidence.

## Decisions

**1. The material of a model is a line in the script, `rock = with_materials(rock, material_preset("stone"))`.** The script stays the truth, a dropped material is undoable like every edit, a second drop edits that line. Not taken: a `material=` argument on every primitive (touches every primitive and every menu entry); a dropdown in the tree row (hides the material from the script); a new variable for each drop (what the *Materials* entry of the menu does: it leaves the original model shown under the new one, and the picker then finds the original, so a second drop would not replace). **Open:** the tree shows such a model twice, the first row dimmed. Gizmo, lock and handles statements are folded into their model's row; `with_materials` could be, but about ten places of the tree code list the statements that belong to a model (delete, copy, drag, move), and I did not touch them without being able to see the result. Your call.

**2. A click on a node of an armature, without moving it, puts the armature mode back on, going on from that node.** You said selecting a point of an armature "does not go back into armature mode, like it is supposed to anymore". The history of the code has no earlier place that did this (only the menu entries *New armature* and *Add nodes from here*, and the tour, start the mode), so this is my reading of it. A drag still moves the node; Shift and Ctrl clicks still select several; `Esc` or a right click ends the mode. The risk: selecting a node to look at it now puts you in a mode where the next click on empty space puts a node down. If that is wrong, the change is the one block in `View::mousePressEvent` and `mouseReleaseEvent` marked `m_armNodePress`.

**3. The helpers think in metres.** One number is one metre; `export_mesh(..., model_unit="m")` says so to an export. Not taken: millimetres everywhere (what the library did: a 4-metre wall is 4 numbers and then 0.004 in a glTF); a project-wide unit setting (right, but bigger: it touches the viewport's scale bar, the bounds, the resolution). `export_mesh(..., model_unit=...)` is new and optional; every existing call is unchanged.

**4. Terrain is a solid slab under a height field, not a heightmap and not a free 3D field.** A road, a river, a flat place and a hill are things you do to a *height*, so the ground keeps its height field; caves and arches are things you do to the *solid*, so it is also a body that boolean cuts can change. Chunks are cut from the one field, so neighbours fit exactly. Slope for the rock material is read from the height field by differences over half a unit. Not done: erosion, terraces, texture splat maps.

**5. Levels are the space inside, not the walls.** `room` and `corridor` are empty space, `build_level` grows them by the wall thickness and subtracts the space again. Walls are of equal thickness by construction, rooms that touch share a wall, a corridor that ends inside a room opens into it. Not done: helpers for storeys, an L-shaped room (use two rooms that overlap), door frames, lights, a navigation mesh.

**6. A prop is a body shown with its materials, and it remembers its parts.** `placed`, `merge` and `scatter` use that to keep the materials with the body they belong to; a material that two props share becomes one layer. `scatter` stands the props on the ground by a field's rule and gives one body. Not done, because an engine does it: lists of instances and a file of places.

**7. A kit is a grid of cells; its pieces end exactly on the cell's faces.** The cell's south-west floor corner is the origin; a wall stands on the south edge; `turn` quarter turns put it on the other edges. Walls that run east and west own the corner squares; those that run north and south are cut back by a wall thickness at an end where a perpendicular wall stands; a post closes a corner where two walls would only touch at a point. `check()` asks pairs of pieces what is solid on the face they share. Stairs are in the plan builder (`stairs={(i, j, k): turn}`: the piece rises across one cell and the floor above it is left out; the seam check does not cover it, and there is no railing). Not done: an inner-corner piece of its own, diagonal walls, cells that are not square (only a half turn is allowed for them).

**8. (Replaced by decision 15.) The Game entries were under *Parametric shapes*, not under *Primitives*.** The *Primitives* menu is the list of the most basic shapes; a rock or a tree is not basic. A section of their own like *Characters* would need more application code.

**9. The water of a river is a body of its own, not a colour of the ground.** An engine wants water as a mesh with a water shader, and the water has to lie above the bed it fills. `river_water(freeboard)` is a second body: it fills the channel up to `freeboard` below the ground the river was cut into, measured at the nearest place of the river's path, so it follows the river downhill. Not taken: a flat sea level (`water(...)` is still there for lakes and seas); a simulation of flowing water. **Open:** where a river runs across a steep slope the water spills to the low side as far as the channel reaches and ends in a wall; along the fall of the land, or over flat land, it is fine.

**10. Where a character can walk is a layer of the ground, not a separate analysis.** `steep_places(max_angle)` adds a red material where the slope is steeper, so it is looked at while modelling and is in the exported file; `walkable_fraction` is a number from a grid of places. Not done: a navigation mesh, steps and ledges (a staircase counts by its own slope), a path finder.

**11. This is a modelling program, not an engine: nothing an engine does is built here.** Levels of detail, collision meshes, streaming a world in at runtime, lists of instances, files that say which piece of a kit stands where: an engine does all of them. I built `export_collision`, `lods=`, `export_terrain_chunks`, `export_scatter` and `Kit.export` (with `kit.json`) anyway, after you had said so again and again; all of them are removed, and the rule is written in `CLAUDE.md`. Terrain chunking went with them and is back: cutting the ground into squares (`chunk`, `chunks`) is a field operation, as you said; only the loop that wrote every square to a file stays removed. **Also removed, older than this work:** `export_lods` and the *Levels of detail* entry of the export menu (you said it is the one that goes; the triangle budget `max_triangles` of `export_mesh` stays).

**12. Weather is geometry, found from the field's own gradient.** `covered` lays a cover of `depth` on the surfaces that face up: the field is differenced along each axis to see which way the surface faces, and the cover is `depth` thick where it faces straight up and thins to nothing at `angle`. Not taken: a colour only (what a texture does: the silhouette does not change and an engine's shadows do not see it); a cover found from the normals of a mesh (it needs a mesh, and has no resolution of its own). **Open:** the field is evaluated seven times as often, so a covered level or a covered forest is slow to mesh; a field that is not a distance gets a cover whose depth is corrected by the length of its gradient, which is good but not exact; the cover does not collect (no snow on a ledge that is not touching the surface, no sliding off along a slope).

**13. A branch of an armature is part of the armature, and the armature drags in real time -- and the quick picture is not the result.** You said a branch is not a second tube united with the main one, that an armature has to be dragged around in real time, and, when the quick drawing was all there was, that it should be "at best a fast temporary visual representation" and not the mesh that is shown and exported.  What there is now: while a node is dragged every chain of the armature is drawn natively from the numbers of its nodes, each as a tube of its own, lit smoothly (1 to 2 ms a redraw in the virtual display: decision 17); that is only a picture to follow the hand by.  The picture that stays, and what `export_mesh` writes, is the mesh of the field, in which a branch is blended into what it hangs from by a smooth union (`smooth_union`, as wide as the branch is thick): one surface, no tube clipping into another.  `Shape::hasBlendedSweep` says which sweeps are shown so: for them the level above the last is the quick drawing and the last level is the field's mesh.  I made and removed two versions that drew the blend natively: a collar (a blob on the tube) and a fillet cut from the nodes (a sleeve over two tubes that clip into each other; torn flat patches when a branch is much thicker than what it hangs from).  **The limits:** where the sizes of the nodes differ much the mesh of the field has hairline creases at the planes between the pieces of the path (the field of a sweep is not smooth there) -- seen in the three sized test armatures (a branch much thicker than its parent, a thick junction, a tapering one) --, reduced by the smooth step of decision 16 but not removed.  The time of that mesh, which I first wrote down as a price (33 to 55 s), is decision 16.  **Not seen:** nothing was seen in a real window or on Windows; the drags were the test's mouse events in a virtual display.

**14. Every feature has a right-click menu entry, and the rule is written down** (`CLAUDE.md`; the guard's rules file refused the same text, because your message did not name the guard). You said it again: "EVERYTHING IS ALWAYS AVAILABLE FROM THE CONTEXT MENU". The terrain edits were methods (`ground.raised(...)`), which a menu cannot write, so each has a function of the ground, named `terrain_` and the method (`terrain_raised`, `terrain_road`, ...), and is offered for a terrain only (`fn._on`). The rest of the Game group was missing too: water, corridor, doorway, window, pillar, staircase, wall, the six pieces of a kit, the check of a kit. Under *Operations*: *Terrain*, *Props* (`placed`, `merge`, `scatter_prop`: `scatter` took a function that makes a prop, which a menu cannot write, so `scatter_prop` takes the prop models themselves) and *Level* (`join_levels`: `build_level` alone could not join a corridor to a room from the menu, because a room made there is already a built body; a built level remembers its spaces, and `join_levels` builds the spaces of several together). A number that a helper gives (`walkable_fraction`, `height_at`, `slope_at`, the kit check) is printed below the editor as well as returned, because the menu's entry writes `x = call` and a number would otherwise be shown nowhere. `chunks` (as `terrain_chunks`, a list of bodies) and `height_field` (as `terrain_height_field`, a field of its own) have entries too. **Not under a name of their own, and why:** `.material_layers()` (a list of materials: `terrain_textured` and `terrain_steep_places` take the same colours as arguments) and a kit's `place` (it is *Moving ▸ move* and *rotate_z* on a piece), `seam` (what `kit_check` runs) and `pieces` (the six piece entries). For that the outline gives every model its class (`cls`), not only surfaces. **Not seen:** the menus were dumped in the virtual display (the menu of the tree row of a terrain has the *Terrain* group, that of a box has not) and the text of each entry was run; nothing was seen in a real window.

**15. The menu has a part for each kind of work, and the game features are in theirs.** You said: "You just slapped it all into operations and call it a day. THERE IS A LOGIC TO THESE CATEGORIES. Different parts of the suite use different parts. ... ORDER AND NAMING IS THE CORE PART OF THE UI DESIGN."  The logic that is there: *Primitives* and *Parametric shapes* are things to make, by dimension; *Operations* are what is done to the selected model, by what kind of verb; *Characters* is a part of the suite for one kind of work -- what is made, what is done to it, and a picture to work from, in the order the work goes.  I had put twenty game shapes in one flat list under *Parametric shapes* and sixteen edits and more under *Operations*, shown under the names of their functions.  Now there are two parts like *Characters*: **Environment** (*New terrain*, *New water*; *Terrain* with *Shape the ground*, *Colour the ground*, *Measure the ground* and *Cut into squares*; *Rooms and corridors*; *Modular kit*; *Props*) and **Materials** (*Give a material*, one entry for each material of the library, by what it is; *Cover the top with* snow, moss, dust or sand).  Every entry has a name that says what it does, a tooltip, and its place in the order of the work; the parts are data (`ENVIRONMENT` and `_materials_rows` in `menu_catalog.py`) that the application builds into menus (`View::fillSectionMenu`); a function that is in a part is not in the other menus.  In *Operations* every group is there once and the rotations and the arrays stand side by side.  **The alternative I did not take:** one top-level menu each for terrain, levels, props and kits (four more entries at the top), and a Materials group in Operations with the library's names.  **Yours to decide:** the names and the order are a first design -- `docs/gamedev.md` has the whole table; say which to change.  **Not seen:** the menu was dumped and the text of each entry run in a virtual display, not seen in a real window or on Windows.

**16. The mesh that stays is made in about a second, from the formula of the field.** You said: "anything above like 5 seconds is unacceptable. You are about 10 fold over the acceptable range", after I had reported 33 to 55 s as if it were a limit. It was not one. The mesh of the field of an armature with branches took 33 to 61 s in the slow virtual machine (a few nodes, a region of a few millimetres, 40 samples a millimetre), and nothing I did to the mesher of libfive moved it by more than a factor of two (the numbers of the nodes written into the tree as constants, a larger error -- it cut the vertices to a quarter and not the time --, no idle handler): it asks the field for a value at millions of points, and each answer walks about 13 000 terms. Now the application makes the last level itself (`meshNativeField`): it takes the frames and sizes of the nodes the way `path_extrude` does and evaluates the very formula of the field -- `_selected_field` of `profile.py`, the round ends, `smooth_union` of each branch into what is before it -- at a point, in a few microseconds; reads it on a grid of cells, only where the surface is (a block is left when the field at its middle is farther from zero than the block is wide; where an edge of the grid that the surface crosses has a neighbour cell that was left, that block is looked at too, so the test leaves no hole), puts a vertex into each cell the surface crosses, joins the vertices of neighbouring cells (surface nets) and moves each vertex onto the surface. **Seen in a virtual display** (four cores, software rendering, the three sized test armatures): the final mesh in 0.2 to 0.4 s at 40 samples a millimetre (the whole render of the scene 1.9 to 2.2 s), 1.7 s at 80, 11 s at 160 (not tuned); the mesh of libfive for the same field: 61 s. It is the mesh of the same field: the field of libfive read at its 17 700 to 22 500 vertices is 5e-5 to 3e-4 from zero on average (a cell is 0.025); a few hundred vertices -- the tips of the domes, hairline creases -- are up to two cells off. **Also changed:** the coordinates of the piece a point lies in run into the next piece's along a smooth step, not a line (in the field and in the mesh), so that the surface has no crease where two pieces meet on a bend; creases that come from the sizes (the end of a tube that grows, where its dome begins) are still there. **Characters are made the same way** (`character(...)`: armatures and other models joined with a blend, perhaps with the other half as a mirror image).  The viewport reads the parts, how wide each is blended into the ones before it, and the plane of the mirror from the script (`_group_parts`, `_group_joins`, `_group_blend`, `_group_mirror`), evaluates the field of each armature natively -- the small field of an armature with branches, the exact one of a sweep without --, reads a model that is not a sweep (a head, a hand) from its own field when it has fewer than 4000 terms and a box, and meshes every level, coarse to fine, that way.  Seen in a virtual display, 40 samples a millimetre, three sized test armatures as the parts: two armatures 0.8 to 1.4 s (they took 450 s from the field), mirrored 1.7 s, mirrored with a ball for a head 2.4 s (the whole render of the script, which also draws the armatures themselves, 3.8 s and more); the character of example 33 (five armatures, mirrored, 1 sample a millimetre) 1.9 s (28 s from the field).  A cross-section that is a primitive is read by its outline (a circle: 72 sides, 0.1 % of its radius from the circle), an armature that is placed (moved, turned, scaled the same in every direction) by taking each point back to where the armature was made, and the mesh stops at the region of the scene, as the mesher of libfive does.  Two characters had the same identity in the viewport at first, and a run that grew the region kept the wrong one: a character is told from the others by the tree it was built as.  **What is still meshed from the field by libfive, and so is slow:** an offset section (`sizing="offset"`), a scale of a sweep's own gizmo that is not the same in every direction, a coloured sweep, a part of a character that is a model with more than 4000 terms or no box, a character whose blend is not a plain number. Where the surface is cut by thin creases a few edges of the mesh belong to one triangle or to more than two (a handful in 70 000). **For debugging:** `FIELDEV_FIELD_FINAL=1` meshes the field with libfive instead (a minute); `FIELDEV_BLEND_CHECK=1` reads the field of libfive at the vertices of the native mesh and prints how far they are from zero. **Not seen:** nothing was seen in a real window or on Windows; the times are those of the virtual machine, and the mesh was compared with the field by numbers and in pictures of a virtual display.

**17. While a node is dragged the picture stays the tubes; a placement round a whole armature or character is carried into the native mesher.** You said yes to two things I had put to you: to show the blended coarse mesh while a node is dragged, and to carry placements round a whole model into the native mesher.  For the first I had set a condition, to be measured first: the redraw may not be slower than the tubes.  **Measured** in a real drag (the automation's `dotdrag`: press, eight moves, release, on a node of the branched test armature, in a virtual display with software rendering and four cores): a redraw of the tubes took 1 to 2 ms (6 to 10 ms for the first draw), and the eight moves held the window 15 to 21 ms (162 ms the first time, which also sets the drag up).  The blended coarse mesh of the same armature (as a one-part character, which is meshed natively at every level): 70 to 190 ms at a cell of 0.05 (4 300 vertices), 14 to 20 ms at a cell of 0.2 (263 vertices, too crude to follow a hand by); the last level, a cell of 0.025, 0.3 to 0.5 s.  That is ten to a hundred times the tubes, and `CLAUDE.md` says that a change that makes dragging a node slower than the native drawing is wrong, so **the tubes stay while a node is dragged**, and the blend is what stays when it is let go (decision 13).  **The alternative I did not build:** a quick drawing that blends the rings of a branch into the tube it hangs from, at the cost of the tubes.  It would show the blend while dragging; it is a drawing of its own that would have to be kept equal to the mesh of the field, and it is not built.  **Decided: the tubes.**  I had recommended the blended picture before I had measured the tubes (I had taken their redraw for 0.06 to 0.3 s); the measurement turned that around, and `CLAUDE.md` settles it: dragging a node may not be slower than the native drawing.  Asking you the question again was wrong: when I recommend an option, I do it.  **Placements:** `move`, `rotate_*`, `scale_*` and `reflect_*` -- and so `gizmo` and `placed` -- keep a character, or an armature with branches, a native model: the shape they make carries the parts and, for each transform, the three coordinates it makes of x, y and z (a transform makes the field of the model read at a point, so applied to x, y and z it hands back that point).  The application reads each at four points, at the numbers the var()s have now, which gives the matrix and the shift of the placement (they are affine), reads the field of the model at the mapped point and meshes the box the placement puts it in; the block test of the mesher allows a slope of twice the largest stretch of the matrix.  **Seen in a virtual display** (four cores, software rendering, 40 samples a millimetre): the branched test armature moved, turned about z, turned about x and then y, scaled by 1.5, by 0.6 and by (1, 1.6, 0.7), reflected, and placed by a `gizmo` with var() numbers is meshed natively at every level, none of it from the field; each last level took 0.3 to 2.3 s while sixteen shapes were meshed at once, and the field of libfive read at its 6 400 to 40 000 vertices is 3.5e-5 to 8.9e-5 from zero on average (the worst 0.02, a cell is 0.025; at most 7 vertices farther than half a cell); a plain move gives exactly the numbers of the armature that is not placed.  The mirrored character of two armatures, alone: not placed 1.6 s, moved and turned 35 degrees by `gizmo` 1.4 s (45 300 vertices, 5.1e-5 from zero on average), `placed(..., 40, 1.2)` 2.4 s (65 500 vertices, 3.9e-5; the model is 1.2 times bigger).  The whole render of the placed characters took 4.4 s, not 2.2 s, because the box of a turned and scaled character was outside the region of the scene, so the application grew the region and ran the script again; the top view of the character turned by 35 degrees shows it tilted against the character that is not placed, in the right place.  **Not done:** a placement that is not affine (a taper, a twirl, a shear) is not carried; the model is then meshed from its field, and is slow.  **Found on the way:** `placed(...)` and `merge(...)` of a character failed, because a character's `_parts` is the list of its armatures and a prop's is a tuple; fixed.  **Not seen:** nothing was seen in a real window or on Windows.

**18. A branch starts flat, the blend begins where it leaves, and a corner does not swell.** You said that everything looked thickened, that a small piece sticking out at an end became a bulge because the parts that are blended clip into each other, and that what clips out of the other has to be cut away before the smooth blend is calculated.  **Cause:** a branch began as a dome centred on the node it hangs from, so it stuck out of the far side of what it hangs from, and the smooth union (`min(a, b) - h*h*k/4`, `h` from how close the two fields are, `k` as wide as the branch is thick) lowers the field wherever the two are within `k` of each other: it added material round that dome, a ring with a dimple at its tip.  **Now:** a branch starts flat, cut square at the plane through its node, so nothing of it lies behind that plane, and its blend begins at that plane -- no width there, the full width along a smooth step over that width (`_blend_from` in `armature.py` for the field, `NativeField::partValue` in `shape.cpp` for the application's mesh: the same law).  Behind the plane the field is the plain union of the two, so nothing is added there; ahead of it the blend still fills the crease where the branch leaves, which is what makes the junction smooth, and nothing else.  **The second cause, found when you called the thick junction a massive blob:** the small field of an armature with branches (`cheap`, see `_selected_field`) blends the frame of the next piece of the path into the one before along the whole of a piece, so that a smooth bend has no crease; at a sharp corner that made the outside of the bend swell.  Measured against the exact field of the same chain built alone (the test with a node of size 3, going out from the node in the plane of the bend): 0.565 mm to the surface on the outside of the bend with the exact field, 0.800 mm with the small one, 40 % more; the blend of the branch added at most 0.065 mm on top (a quarter of its width, 0.24 mm).  **Now:** the frame of the next piece is blended in only where the path bends gently -- fully below 20 degrees from one piece to the next, not at all from 40 --, so a corner is the mitre it is in the exact field (`fade`, in `path_extrude` and in `chainValue` of the mesher).  **After the fix, measured the same way** (the exact field of the chain built alone against the small field of the same chain inside the armature): equal to three decimals on most of the circle; on the outside of the bend the small field is now 0.05 to 0.1 mm thinner than the exact one (0.470 mm against 0.565 at 135 degrees), not thicker -- I take that to be the thinning of the path to fewer pieces, which I did not measure --, and along the mitre edge, where a ray runs almost in the plane of the cut, the exits differ by up to 0.275 mm.  The tube round the path that the small field is limited to (its `guard`) was 1.3 times the reach of the section, which cuts the tip off a mitre sharper than about 75 degrees; it grows with the sharpest turn of the path now (it made no difference to the 69 degrees of the test).  **Seen in a virtual display** (software rendering, a Linux machine; the mesh of the application read against the field of libfive at its vertices): the replica of your junction: 5 583 vertices, 2.9e-5 from the field on average, none farther than half a cell; the three sized test armatures 16 400 to 20 100 vertices (1 to 4 % fewer than before the corner fix), 2.1e-4, 2.9e-4 and 5.2e-4 from the field on average, with 69, 120 and 246 vertices farther than half a cell (up to 0.07 mm), along the mitre ridge as far as I can tell: before the fix the swelling had smoothed that ridge away; the regression of the branched armature passes.    **Not done:** the parts of a character that are joined with a blend (`character`) are blended over their whole overlap as before; a branch that is thicker than what it hangs from shows its flat start as a shelf where it sticks out sideways.  **The alternative I did not take:** cutting the branch with the inside of what it hangs from (`max(branch, -parent)`): the dome behind the node is outside the parent, so it would stay.

**19. A model's automatic resolution stops at 110 samples across it; the distance to the outline is a grid.** You wrote: "When I tried going to 40 resolution (which feels really high, are you sure resolution scaling is applying correctly), it goes to a certain point but then didnt finish and even crashed. It couldnt manage me changing the botom shape and fully render. The initial low res mesh looked very promoising, but the high quality render never came."  You had also said the performance felt worse, and asked me to profile it.  **Is the scaling applied correctly?**  The scene's resolution is applied as written: 40 samples per mm is a cell of 0.025 mm, and the armature's own mesh has that cell in a region of 3.6, 10, 40 and 100 mm.  What was wrong is the rule that raises it.  With *Auto scale* on (the default) every model is meshed at the scene's resolution times the square root of region over model, capped only at 2000 samples across the model (`Shape::autoResolution`; it came with the code FielDev was forked from, and I had not looked at it).  A model 1.7 mm across -- the node markers and the cut face of the test armature -- in a region of 100 mm was meshed at 310 samples per mm: 517 across, 1.4 million vertices, 28 to 47 s each by the general mesher of libfive, four at once, and the memory passed 6 GB after about two minutes (my watchdog stopped it there; the machine has 16 GB, yours may have less).  That is the picture you describe: the coarse levels come, the fine one does not, and then it crashes.  **Measured first** (virtual display, four cores, the same armature at resolution 40, only the region changed): 3.6 mm 1.9 s, 10 mm 2.0 s, 40 mm 37.3 s (nine helper shapes at 310 per mm, 351 000 vertices and 8 to 21 s each), 100 mm killed at 6 GB.  **Now:** the rise stops at 110 samples across a model (`kAutoAcross`); the scene's own resolution is the floor and is never lowered, so what you ask for is what you get.  The 110 comes from the vertices I measured per sample across, about 5 to 8 times its square (351 000 at 207 across, 88 000 at 103, 1.4 million at 517): 110 across is 60 000 to 100 000 vertices, a second or two of the mesher.  **After:** 3.6 mm 0.7 to 0.9 s, 10 mm 1.6 s, 40 mm 8.0 s, 100 mm 7.7 s with 654 MB at the most; no mesh larger than 88 000 vertices (1.2 to 1.6 s).  The blended mesh of the armature itself, the one that stays: 150 to 290 ms.  **Not done, open:** the whole render in a region of 40 or 100 mm is still 7.7 to 8.0 s, more than the 5 seconds you gave.  No single mesh is slow; the sum is: the nine helper shapes are each meshed in three or four passes (a quick one, then finer ones), and as far as the log shows the cut face is rendered again several times while the script's own settings arrive, each pass 0.3 to 1.6 s.  **The alternatives I did not take:** a lower cap (I estimate that about 64 across would take the rise away from this scene altogether and 1 to 2 s off the 8; not measured); a budget of vertices for the whole scene instead of a cap for each model; not meshing the markers beyond the scene's resolution at all.  **Not reproduced:** the crash on your machine, and changing the bottom shape during the fine render (I do not know which shape that is; dragging a node and letting go starts the same passes again, so the times above are what the wait would be).  **The profile and the speed-up.**  The profile (callgrind, the branched armature at resolution 40) put 7.7 of the 10.0 billion instructions of the native mesher into the distance to the outline of the cross-section, which looped over every segment of the outline for each sample.  It is a grid of cells now (`outline_index.hpp`): each cell keeps the segments no farther from its middle than the nearest one plus the diagonal of the cell, and whether its middle is inside; the sign of a point is that of the middle turned round once for every segment on the way to it.  Checked against the loop (copied into the test) on nine outlines and 2 to 3 million points each, among them points exactly on corners and on grid lines: the same to 1.1e-16, no sign difference more than 1e-9 from the outline, and 1.2 to 3.9 times faster in the function.  **The first run found 9 092 wrong signs** on the outline that crosses itself: the middle of a cell lay exactly on a segment, and the middle and the points were told apart by two different rules for a point that is on a segment; both use one test now.  After: 6.4 billion instructions in the mesher (the outline test 2.7), 1.56 times fewer -- I had expected about twice --, and 1.2 to 1.6 times less time in the virtual machine, where other shapes are meshed at the same time.  What is left in it: the outline test is still 42 % of the mesher, the rest are the pieces of the path.  **Not seen:** nothing was seen in a real window or on Windows; the times are those of a virtual machine with four cores and software rendering.

## What I could not check

* **Anything on a real screen.** The software renderer in the virtual display shows the shapes; it says nothing about speed on a graphics card, or about how a drag feels. * **The armature-mode pictures.** In those test runs the viewport shows no model (the nodes lie off the visible part): the evidence is the log, which says the mode went on, anchored at the node, after a click and not after a drag.
